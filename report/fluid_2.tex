Comparing the three meshing approaches reveals notable differences among the resulting coefficients. The coarse mesh, showing considerable deviation from the medium and fine mesh results, does not provide the required accuracy and reliability, and is therefore not a suitable choice.

On the other hand, while the fine mesh offers the highest level of accuracy, it comes with challenges such as long computational time and high CPU demand, which limit its practical applicability.

Given the above, the medium mesh emerges as the best option. This approach strikes a balance between adequate accuracy and reasonable computational time, offering an efficient and optimal solution. All subsequent simulations in this report therefore use the medium mesh.

\subsection{Flow Analysis for the Two Regimes}

When the flow regime around the airfoil transitions from turbulent to laminar, the reduced stability of the laminar flow causes the boundary layer to separate from the airfoil surface earlier. This premature separation leads to several adverse effects on airfoil performance:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Increased pressure drag and drag coefficient:} Early boundary-layer separation substantially increases pressure drag and, consequently, the drag coefficient of the airfoil.
    \item \textbf{Reduced lift force and lift coefficient:} Since a larger portion of the flow separates from the airfoil surface and no longer contributes to lift generation, the lift force -- and hence the lift coefficient -- decreases.
    \item \textbf{Reduced flow velocity near the surface:} This change in flow regime is accompanied by a decrease in fluid velocity near the airfoil surface, which further intensifies the separation phenomenon.
\end{itemize}

Overall, the transition of the flow regime from turbulent to laminar around the airfoil, due to the effects described above, significantly degrades the aerodynamic performance of the airfoil.

\section{In-Depth Analysis of Flow Physics}

\subsection{Comparison of $C_l$ and $C_d$ at Two Different Angles of Attack}

At the initial angle of attack of $0^\circ$:
\begin{align*}
C_l &= 0.1880 \\
C_d &= 0.0107
\end{align*}

At the secondary angle of attack of $8^\circ$:
\begin{align*}
C_l &= 0.9349 \\
C_d &= 0.0227
\end{align*}

Increasing the angle of attack from $0^\circ$ to $8^\circ$, while keeping other parameters such as Reynolds number and freestream velocity constant, leads to the following changes in the aerodynamic performance of the airfoil:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Increased lift coefficient:} As the angle of attack increases, the component of the aerodynamic force perpendicular to the flow also increases. This means greater lift generation and, consequently, a higher lift coefficient.
    \item \textbf{Increased drag coefficient:} Simultaneously with the increase in lift, the drag coefficient also rises. This is due to increased flow separation on the upper surface of the airfoil, which increases drag.
    \item \textbf{Proximity to the stall point:} Although an angle of attack of $8^\circ$ still lies within the linear lift range, it is closer to the stall point, resulting in a more unsteady and turbulent flow.
    \item \textbf{Overall performance improvement:} In general, the aerodynamic performance of the airfoil improves. However, this improvement continues only up to the point where the increase in drag does not cause a significant drop in the lift-to-drag ratio.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Analysis:}

\textit{At $0^\circ$ angle of attack:} The static pressure distribution over the two airfoil surfaces is relatively symmetric. The pressure difference between the upper and lower surfaces is not substantial; only a slight decrease in pressure on the upper surface and a slight increase on the lower surface are observed. This condition indicates negligible or near-zero lift generation.

\textit{At $8^\circ$ angle of attack:} With the increase in angle of attack to $8^\circ$, significant changes occur in the pressure contours. On the upper surface (the suction region), a considerable pressure decrease is observed, while the lower surface experiences a greater pressure increase. This growing pressure differential between the two surfaces directly leads to an increase in lift force.

Additionally, at this higher angle of attack, the steeper pressure gradient on the upper surface may indicate the onset of partial flow separation. Although this phenomenon has not yet reached a critical stage at $8^\circ$, it suggests an increased likelihood of flow instability and proximity to stall conditions.

\textit{At $8^\circ$ angle of attack:} With the increase in angle of attack to $8^\circ$, the dynamic pressure on the upper surface -- particularly near the leading edge -- shows a significant increase. This reflects a substantially higher velocity and stronger suction in this region, which contributes to lift generation.

However, as the trailing portion of the upper surface is approached, a drop in dynamic pressure may indicate the onset of flow separation. Even if minor, this phenomenon can lead to flow instability and, ultimately, a reduction in airfoil efficiency.

\textit{At $8^\circ$ angle of attack:} With the angle of attack increased to $8^\circ$, noticeable changes occur in the velocity contour:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Increased velocity on the upper surface:} The velocity on the upper airfoil surface, particularly near the leading edge, increases noticeably. This velocity increase is directly associated with the pressure drop and greater lift generation.
    \item \textbf{Decreased velocity on the lower surface:} Simultaneously, the velocity on the lower surface of the airfoil decreases.
    \item \textbf{Signs of flow separation:} In the trailing portion of the upper surface, regions of reduced velocity are observed, which may indicate the onset of flow separation. Although this phenomenon may be minor at this angle, it serves as a warning of possible instabilities at higher angles of attack.
\end{itemize}
